COVID deaths and unemployment: two shocks, two timelines

Did the states hit hardest by COVID also lose the most jobs? The story runs in steps: the shock, the lag, the health channel, the cost, the map, the economic channel, and the test across states.

1. When did the deaths come?

U.S. · monthly, Jan 2020–Dec 2025

What it shows: Deaths came in waves. The peak month was January 2021 with about 120,500 deaths. After 2022 each wave was much smaller.

Limits: provisional CDC counts by week, summed to months by week-ending date. Recent weeks may be revised.

2. Did jobs and output recover as deaths fell?

U.S. · yearly, 2020–2025 · unemployment and real GDP growth are simple averages of the 50 states

What it shows: Unemployment peaked first (7.3% in 2020) and deaths peaked a year later (about 470,000 in 2021). By 2022 unemployment had fallen to 3.3%, even though 2022 still saw about 256,000 deaths. The labor market recovered faster than the death toll fell. Real GDP growth followed the same path: –2.2% in 2020, then +5.7% in 2021.

Limits: the state average is unweighted, so small states count as much as large ones. Yearly averages hide the April 2020 spike.

3. Did hospitals feel it the way deaths did?

All 50 states · Medicare beneficiaries · 2021 · national trend 2020–2022

What it shows: States with more Medicare COVID hospitalizations per 100,000 also had more COVID deaths per 100,000 (correlation about 0.84 in 2021). Nationally, Medicare COVID hospitalizations rose from about 987,000 in 2020 to about 1.17 million in 2022, while the average stay shortened from 10.7 to 9.1 days.

Limits: Medicare covers mostly people 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities, while deaths cover all ages. The Medicare file has full years for 2020–2022 and only January–June for 2023 (about 282,500 national stays, 8.2-day average stay), which is left out of the yearly views. It has no dollar costs; panel 4 adds an estimate.

4. What did it cost?

U.S. · Medicare beneficiaries · yearly, 2020–2022 · an estimate, not an observed total

Limits: each estimate is the number of Medicare COVID stays (CMS) times a published average cost per stay: $11,267 (median hospital cost, U.S. hospital study, 2020 data), $21,752 (mean cost, Medicare fee-for-service, Tsai et al. 2021), and $24,826 (mean cost, adult patients, all payers, Mar 2020–Jul 2021). The same price is applied to every year, but stays got shorter, so 2021 and 2022 are probably overstated. The low figure is a median and the other two are means. Medicare patients only.

6. Did output fall where jobs fell?

All 50 states · 2020 · real GDP growth vs. unemployment rate

What it shows: Yes, in 2020. States with larger GDP drops had higher unemployment (correlation about –0.48). The link faded to about zero in 2021–2023, so the economic shock was mainly a 2020 event.

Limits: annual figures. Industry mix (tourism, energy) drives the biggest swings. Correlation is not cause.

7. Did more deaths mean more job damage?

All 50 states · total deaths per 100,000 (2020–2025) vs. change in unemployment rate, 2020 to 2025

What it shows: There is no clear pattern. The correlation is about –0.02, and the fitted line is almost flat: 100 more deaths per 100,000 goes with about 0.03 points less unemployment change. Hawaii and Mississippi sit at opposite ends of deaths, yet job outcomes depend on other things.

Limits: correlation is not cause. Industry mix (tourism vs. energy vs. manufacturing), lockdown timing, and federal relief all differ by state. Fifty states is a small sample.

8. Reading the numbers

Takeaway: COVID hit lives, hospitals, and jobs on different timelines. Within each channel the measures moved together across states: deaths and hospitalizations (correlation about 0.84), and GDP and unemployment in 2020 (about –0.48). Between the channels they did not line up: a state’s death toll did not predict its job loss (about –0.02) or its 2020 GDP change (about –0.08). The economic shock was sharp in 2020 and largely over by 2021–2022; the death toll peaked later. Medicare hospital stays alone cost an estimated $37–$81 billion over 2020–2022 (middle estimate about $71 billion).
